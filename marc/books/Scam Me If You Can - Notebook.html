1. The Playbook Exposed: Recognize—and Beat—the Con Artist’s Game
Highlight (yellow) - Page 24
Con men like Kevin are well trained in the techniques to get targets “under the ether.” Ether is a condition of trust and even infatuation with what is being presented.
Highlight (yellow) - Page 24
To get their victims under the ether, fraudsters hit their fear, panic, and urgency buttons.
Highlight (yellow) - Page 25
Nancy answered Kevin’s call because it looked like it came from a local Florida area code, not an 800 number, like many previous calls she’d received (and hadn’t answered). The area code made her feel that the call was legitimate.
Highlight (yellow) - Page 26
The best con artists know how to use a comforting and confident tone of voice to get people under the ether. They prey on vulnerabilities and seduce their victims into revealing their personal information. I perfected this technique while running scams as a young man. I knew how to modulate my voice and speak in a calm and reassuring manner to all my targets. The effect was almost hypnotic—

a good con should always try “to keep the victim up in the altitude of the ether, because once they drop into the valley of logic, [you’ve] lost them.”
Highlight (yellow) - Page 26
To induce the ether, the con artist asks questions that trigger emotional responses.
Highlight (yellow) - Page 26
asking Nancy about her concerns about the cost of the unit and whether she would be able to carve out enough time to use it that it would be worthwhile in the future.
Highlight (yellow) - Page 26
In a home improvement scam, the con might ask the victim if it has been frustrating to deal with needed repairs. Once a con identifies your triggers, he uses them as part of the pitch to drive you to a heightened emotional state.
Highlight (yellow) - Page 27
Instead of asking, “What’s your monthly mortgage payment?” which would put the target on edge, the con might gently say, “Let me ask you something. It sounds like you have a wonderful home there. How much is that mortgage each month?” Instead of “When did your spouse die?” he said, “I know you loved your husband very much and miss him terribly. If you don’t mind my asking, how long has he been gone?” Once the emotions and memories come flooding into the target’s mind, the target connects those feelings to the person on the other end of the phone. The con has become a “friend,” so the target opens up, answering personal questions and, more important, starting to trust the con.
Highlight (yellow) - Page 28
HOW TO AVOID TRUSTING SOMEONE YOU SHOULDN’T Never engage a stranger in dialogue about your personal life. Don’t reveal any personal information— not just account information but also names of family, friends, and even pets. Every time a stranger asks you a personal question, ask this question in return: Why do you need to know? Remember that you don’t owe anything to a stranger— not information, consent to buy what they are selling, or anything else.

HITTING YOUR SCARCITY AND URGENCY BUTTONS, WITH A TOUCH OF FLATTERY THROWN IN
Highlight (yellow) - Page 28
Psychology professor Robert Cialdini popularized the term “scarcity principle” to explain how people are enticed by opportunities that seem finite or fleeting. Scarcity is often paired with urgency (You must act now!) and flattery (You’re too smart to let a deal this great get away!).
Highlight (yellow) - Page 30
TEN WAYS TO AVOID THE TRIPLE THREAT: SCARCITY, URGENCY, AND FLATTERY Check your emotions. If you are approached with a pitch to win a large amount of money or become part of a limited- time- only investment opportunity or send money to someone you haven’t met, pay attention to your reaction. Is your heart racing? Are you excited? Do you start dreaming of all the things you could do with the money? These kinds of reactions are signs that you may be under the ether. Don’t make a decision in this emotional state. Give it twenty- four hours. Make it a nonnegotiable personal rule to give yourself one full day to think about any financial decision. This will diminish the emotion attached to the pitch and give you time to think without any pressure from anyone else. Practice introspection. As enticing as the offer may sound, pull yourself back into logic mode by asking yourself a few questions, including: What’s in this deal for the person asking? How does it benefit him? Does it make sense that I am getting this offer? Why me? How will this offer benefit me in the long run? Are there better, more reliable, or familiar alternatives to this offer? Keep it close to the vest. Don’t answer personal questions, especially about your family situation, finances, or worries. Don’t share personal information with people you don’t know or haven’t met in person. Get nosy. Always ask more questions than you answer. Put the caller on the spot with questions, including details about the offer, why the person called you, his or her credentials and street address, and so on. Don’t fall for time limits. When approached with an offer that is available for a limited time or is in limited supply, remind yourself that limited- time offers are rarely legitimate. Even supermarkets put grocery items on sale in weekly rotation! If you don’t get the eggs on sale this week, you can get them on sale in a few weeks. Do due diligence. Take the time to research the offer, the person, or the company. Read reviews. I always look at a seller’s feedback in forums and on consumer websites. Check ratings with business rating organizations and consumer groups. Evaluate any complaints or negative feedback about the company or seller. When it comes to financial investments, such as purchasing currency like coins, don’t risk it if there’s even one negative complaint. Tell someone. If you or anyone you know is being harassed by someone you believe to be a con artist (or by anyone, for that matter), contact law enforcement and file a report. Don’t pick up. You don’t have to answer your calls. Screen them, and don’t call back. Stay informed.
